Being right and being helpful are not the same thing. Somewhere along the way, modern customer service appears to have forgotten the distinction.

Customer service should be relatively straightforward. A customer raises a concern, a business identifies the issue, and the appropriate person responds with an explanation, resolution or acknowledgement.

Yet customer service can sometimes feel less like a conversation and more like an exercise in corporate self-defence.

Policies are repeated. Carefully constructed expressions of empathy are inserted into responses. Decisions are justified. Tickets are closed. Satisfaction surveys are distributed.

But is anyone actually listening?

A recent personal experience with EatClub, an Australian dining deals platform, provides an interesting example of how customer service can lose sight of its purpose — and what businesses, software developers and customer experience teams can learn from it.

## Being right does not make you good at customer service

My original interaction with [EatClub](https://eatclub.com.au/) concerned a cashback promotion involving [Asahi](https://www.asahibeverages.com/).

EatClub declined the claim, citing promotional eligibility requirements, submission deadlines and the absence of a qualifying offer associated with my transaction.

Whether or not I agreed with that decision is largely irrelevant to the subject of this article.

What caught my attention was how the company concluded its response.

Having explained why I would not receive the cashback, EatClub immediately suggested that I participate in another promotion involving [Hugo Spritz](https://www.stgermainliqueur.com/cocktails/hugo-spritz/).

There is something fundamentally disconnected about telling an already dissatisfied customer that their previous promotional experience has ended unsuccessfully, before encouraging them to participate in another promotion.

It may have been intended as a helpful suggestion. However, from my perspective as the customer, it came across as an attempt to generate further business rather than address dissatisfaction.

My subsequent response focused on that behaviour.

I questioned whether a department branding itself as the Customer Happiness Team should be introducing new promotional opportunities while dealing with an unhappy customer.

In that subsequent reply, I did not request that the promotional terms be overridden or demand preferential treatment. I was challenging the quality of the customer service interaction itself.

## Responding to an argument that nobody made

EatClub responded by explaining its responsibility to apply promotional terms fairly and consistently.

It explained that customer happiness did not mean overriding eligibility requirements and that it could not guarantee a favourable outcome in every situation.

All reasonable statements in isolation.

But they were not addressing the central argument I had made.

Within the response was an important acknowledgement: EatClub accepted that introducing another promotion while I was raising an unresolved concern was inappropriate, and apologised for the timing.

That acknowledgement could have formed the entire basis of a successful response.

Instead, it was surrounded by further explanations defending the original promotional decision.

This illustrates an important distinction that businesses can easily overlook.

**The original reason a customer contacts a business is not necessarily the reason they continue contacting it.**

A conversation can evolve.

A billing enquiry can become a complaint about communication. A technical support request can become a complaint about staff conduct. A promotional dispute can become a complaint about customer service.

Continuing to respond to the original issue without recognising that the customer's concern has changed creates unnecessary friction.

## When customer service finally listens

After receiving that response, I clarified that my complaint concerned the way the support team had handled my dissatisfaction, not a request for an exception to the promotional conditions.

On 26 November 2026, EatClub responded again. This time, the company explicitly recognised the distinction. It acknowledged that its previous response had revisited an issue I was not disputing, accepted that it had missed the point of my feedback again, and apologised for adding to my frustration. It also indicated that it would take my feedback on board.

That deserves recognition. The final response addressed the actual complaint without repeating the eligibility arguments or offering a new promotion. No compensation or reversal of the cashback decision was offered, and neither was necessary to address this particular criticism.

The problem was that it took an additional exchange to reach that point.

For businesses, this is an example of both a communication failure and a constructive recovery. The final acknowledgement demonstrates that an organisation can accept responsibility for its communication without conceding that its original transactional decision was wrong.

Whether the feedback leads to changes in EatClub's internal support processes remains unknown. But the final response itself was a meaningful improvement.

## Understanding words is not the same as understanding intent

In software engineering, we often distinguish between receiving information and interpreting it correctly.

Customer service faces a similar challenge.

Consider two separate customer intentions:

- **Intent A:** I want you to reconsider your decision regarding my cashback claim.
- **Intent B:** I want you to acknowledge that promoting another offer during a complaint was inappropriate.

These are fundamentally different requests.

The first concerns transactional eligibility. The second concerns customer experience and professional judgement.

They require different responses, different reasoning and potentially different internal workflows.

Treating both as the same complaint creates a situation in which a business may provide technically accurate information while missing the purpose of the conversation.

For organisations investing in customer relationship management platforms, helpdesk software and artificial intelligence, this distinction should be particularly important.

A system that can retrieve the correct policy but cannot recognise a change in customer intent is only solving part of the problem.

## The problem with performative empathy

Modern customer service frequently relies on familiar expressions:

- We understand your frustration.
- We acknowledge your disappointment.
- Your experience matters to us.
- We appreciate your feedback.
- Thank you for your understanding.

None of these statements are inherently problematic.

The problem arises when they become substitutes for demonstrating actual understanding.

Telling a customer that you understand their frustration while responding to a different concern creates a contradiction. The words communicate empathy, but the behaviour does not support them.

There is also something presumptuous about thanking a customer for their understanding when they have not indicated that they accept the explanation.

Understanding a company's position does not necessarily mean agreeing with it, accepting it or considering it satisfactory.

Empathy should be demonstrated through the relevance and quality of a response, not merely declared through carefully selected language.

A straightforward acknowledgement of the specific issue can be considerably more effective than several paragraphs of carefully constructed corporate reassurance.

## Customer support is not a marketing channel

Businesses understandably want to retain customers, encourage further transactions and promote new products.

However, timing matters.

A customer support interaction should not automatically become another opportunity to advertise.

Consider the following scenarios:

| Customer complaint | Inappropriate response |
| --- | --- |
| A disputed cashback promotion | Advertising another cashback promotion |
| An unexpected subscription charge | Suggesting a premium subscription |
| A failed product delivery | Promoting another product |
| Unreliable internet service | Encouraging a more expensive plan without diagnosing the issue |
| A poor restaurant experience | Advertising another dining offer |

These recommendations may be commercially relevant, but their timing can make them counterproductive.

Businesses should consider separating complaint handling from promotional activity, particularly when the customer has expressed dissatisfaction.

From a software design perspective, this is not necessarily a complicated problem.

A customer relationship management system could implement rules to suppress promotional suggestions when an account has an active complaint or unresolved escalation.

Marketing opportunities should not take precedence over restoring customer confidence.

## When businesses become too concerned with defending themselves

Organisations need policies. They need consistency, safeguards against abuse and processes that ensure customers are treated fairly.

But there is a difference between explaining a policy and repeatedly defending it.

A business does not need to concede that its original decision was incorrect to acknowledge that its handling of the situation could have been better.

These two statements can coexist:

- Our original decision was consistent with our terms and conditions.
- Our subsequent communication was inappropriate.

Acknowledging the second does not invalidate the first.

When businesses struggle to separate these concepts, customer service risks becoming an exercise in protecting the organisation's position rather than improving the customer's experience.

The result can be an unnecessarily adversarial relationship between the business and its customers.

Not every criticism requires a defence. Not every dissatisfied customer is requesting compensation. Not every complaint is an attempt to circumvent company policy.

Sometimes, a customer is simply pointing out that something could have been handled better.

## Where artificial intelligence fits into the problem

AI is increasingly being incorporated into customer service platforms.

It can classify support requests, summarise conversations, retrieve policies, recommend responses and identify escalation opportunities.

Used effectively, it could help prevent exactly the type of communication problem described in this article.

For example, an AI-assisted support system could identify that a customer's latest message concerns staff communication rather than the original transactional dispute.

It could then recommend that the response focus on acknowledging the communication issue instead of repeating previously supplied eligibility information.

However, AI could equally make the problem worse.

If a system is designed primarily to defend business policies, minimise compensation, reduce handling time or close support tickets, it may become exceptionally efficient at producing responses that sound professional without actually resolving the customer's concern.

AI does not automatically improve customer service.

It can amplify the processes, objectives and assumptions embedded within the systems using it.

There is no evidence available to me that EatClub used AI or automation to generate the correspondence discussed here. The example simply illustrates the kind of contextual problem that technology could either address or amplify.

## Designing better customer support systems

For businesses developing their own customer support processes or integrating existing helpdesk platforms, several practical improvements are worth considering.

### Track changing customer intent

Do not permanently classify a conversation according to its original subject.

A customer's latest message may introduce an entirely different concern requiring separate handling.

Support systems should allow agents to distinguish between:

- The original transaction or support request.
- The customer's current concern.
- Previously communicated decisions.
- Unresolved complaints.
- Subsequent escalations.

This allows a conversation to evolve without losing its historical context.

### Separate policy enforcement from service quality

A transactional decision and a customer service complaint should be capable of independent assessment.

An organisation can maintain its original decision while acknowledging shortcomings in communication or professional conduct.

A helpdesk system should support this distinction rather than treating every subsequent message as a challenge to the original decision.

### Suppress promotional messaging during complaints

Introduce safeguards that prevent marketing recommendations from appearing in sensitive customer support interactions.

Promotional messaging should be contextually appropriate, not simply commercially available.

This can be implemented as a business rule rather than relying entirely on an employee or AI system to exercise appropriate judgement.

For example, promotional suggestions could be disabled when:

- A customer has an unresolved complaint.
- A support ticket has been escalated.
- A customer has explicitly expressed dissatisfaction.
- A refund or billing dispute remains unresolved.
- A trust and safety investigation is active.

These safeguards should apply to automated recommendations and agent-facing suggestions alike.

### Avoid unnecessary repetition

Before restating a policy or explanation, determine whether the customer has actually challenged it.

Repeating information that has already been communicated does not necessarily provide additional clarity.

It can instead make the customer feel ignored.

A support system should help agents understand what has already been explained, what the customer has acknowledged and what remains outstanding.

### Measure understanding, not just resolution

Traditional customer service metrics often include:

- First response time.
- Average handling time.
- Ticket closure rate.
- Customer satisfaction scores.
- Escalation frequency.

These are useful metrics, but they do not necessarily establish whether the customer's concern was correctly understood.

Organisations should also consider measuring:

| Metric | What it reveals |
| --- | --- |
| Intent alignment | Whether the response addressed the customer's latest stated concern |
| Customer repetition rate | How often customers needed to repeat or clarify their complaint |
| Misunderstanding-related reopen rate | How often tickets were reopened because the actual issue was missed |
| Unnecessary policy repetition | Whether agents repeated explanations unrelated to the current concern |
| Escalation avoidance | Whether appropriate communication prevented unnecessary escalation |
| Inappropriate promotional exposure | Whether customers received marketing suggestions during unresolved complaints |

A ticket being closed does not necessarily mean the customer's concern has been resolved.

It may simply mean the customer has stopped trying.

## Implementing contextual safeguards in software

Developers building customer support integrations can introduce explicit business rules before an agent or AI system generates a response.

A simplified C# example might look like this:

```csharp
public enum SupportIntent
{
    GeneralEnquiry,
    TransactionDispute,
    TechnicalSupport,
    ServiceComplaint,
    SafetyIncident
}

public sealed record SupportContext(
    SupportIntent CurrentIntent,
    bool HasUnresolvedComplaint,
    bool IsEscalated,
    bool HasExpressedDissatisfaction,
    bool PolicyAlreadyExplained,
    bool CustomerChallengesPolicy
);

public static class SupportResponseRules
{
    public static bool AllowPromotion(SupportContext context) =>
        context.CurrentIntent == SupportIntent.GeneralEnquiry
        && !context.HasUnresolvedComplaint
        && !context.IsEscalated
        && !context.HasExpressedDissatisfaction;

    public static bool ShouldExplainPolicy(SupportContext context) =>
        context.CustomerChallengesPolicy
        || (
            context.CurrentIntent == SupportIntent.TransactionDispute
            && !context.PolicyAlreadyExplained
        );

    public static bool RequiresServiceReview(SupportContext context) =>
        context.CurrentIntent == SupportIntent.ServiceComplaint;
}
```

This is deliberately simple and illustrative rather than a complete production implementation.

The important architectural decision is to separate business rules from response generation.

An AI model or customer service agent should not need to independently determine whether promotional messaging is appropriate every time a response is prepared.

The system can establish that boundary.

Similarly, whether a previous policy explanation should be repeated can depend on the current customer intent rather than the original ticket classification.

In production, these classifications should remain open to human review and correction. An incorrectly classified complaint can create the same problems as having no contextual safeguards at all.

## Give AI explicit contextual instructions

When using AI to assist customer service agents, consider instructions such as:

> Identify the primary concern expressed in the customer's most recent message.
>
> Determine whether this differs from the original support request.
>
> Address the current concern directly.
>
> Distinguish dissatisfaction with a business decision from dissatisfaction with how that decision was communicated.
>
> Do not repeat previously supplied policy explanations unless they remain relevant to the current concern.
>
> Do not assume that dissatisfaction represents a request for compensation, an exception or preferential treatment.
>
> Do not introduce promotional recommendations while a customer complaint remains unresolved.
>
> If the customer's concern is ambiguous, seek clarification rather than constructing an unsupported interpretation.

These instructions are not a substitute for proper system design, reliable classification or human oversight.

However, they provide a useful starting point for developing AI-assisted support workflows that prioritise contextual understanding.

## Testing whether your support system actually listens

Businesses already test software functionality, payment processing, authentication and other operational workflows.

Customer service workflows should receive similar attention.

Consider introducing acceptance tests for customer support behaviour.

| Scenario | Expected behaviour |
| --- | --- |
| Customer disputes a promotional decision | Explain the relevant eligibility requirements |
| Customer subsequently complains about staff communication | Address the communication complaint separately |
| Customer expresses dissatisfaction with an unresolved issue | Suppress unrelated promotional recommendations |
| Customer explicitly requests reconsideration | Review or explain the original decision |
| Customer acknowledges a policy but criticises its communication | Do not unnecessarily repeat the policy |
| Customer raises multiple independent concerns | Address each concern separately |
| Customer intent cannot be confidently determined | Request clarification or escalate for human review |

These scenarios can be used for manual quality assurance, automated business-rule testing or the evaluation of AI-generated support responses.

The objective is not to force every customer interaction into a predefined script.

It is to ensure that the system recognises meaningful differences between customer intentions and responds accordingly.

## What the first response to my feedback could have looked like

Before the additional clarification became necessary, a more appropriate response to my customer service feedback might have been:

> Thank you for clarifying your concern. We acknowledge that suggesting another promotion in our previous response was inappropriate given the circumstances.
>
> While our original decision regarding the Asahi promotion remains unchanged, we recognise that your feedback concerns how we communicated that decision rather than the eligibility requirements themselves.
>
> We apologise for the way this interaction was handled and appreciate you bringing it to our attention.

This response does not override promotional terms.

It does not offer compensation.

It does not create an exception or undermine the consistent application of company policy.

It simply demonstrates that the business has understood the criticism.

Sometimes, that is all a customer is asking for. EatClub’s eventual response demonstrated this principle in practice.

## Final thoughts

Customer service should not be measured solely by how effectively a business defends its decisions.

It should also be measured by how effectively it listens, understands, communicates and responds to the people using its products and services.

Corporate language, sophisticated helpdesk platforms and artificial intelligence cannot compensate for a fundamental failure to understand what a customer is actually saying.

Businesses do not need to agree with every customer. They do not need to provide every requested refund, compensation payment or exception.

But they should recognise that being technically correct does not excuse poor communication.

EatClub’s eventual acknowledgement illustrates why businesses should make contextual understanding part of their support processes from the outset, rather than relying on customers to repeatedly clarify their concerns. A constructive recovery matters, but preventing avoidable friction matters too.

**Good customer service is not about winning an argument. It is about understanding why the conversation is happening in the first place.**
